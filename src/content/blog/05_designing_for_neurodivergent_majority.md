# Designing for the Neurodivergent Majority: Why Time Tracking Tools Fail Half the Creative Industry

*48% of creative professionals identify as neurodivergent. Every time tracking tool on the market was designed for the other 52%. That's not an accessibility gap. It's a market failure.*

---

In 2025, Understood.org, the 4A's, and Havas published a landmark study on neurodiversity in the creative industries. The headline finding: **48% of creative industry professionals identify as neurodivergent**, compared to 31% of the general population.

Nearly half. Not a minority to accommodate. Not an edge case to consider. Half of your creative department.

And among specific conditions, the numbers are even more striking. Seven percent of adults in the creative sector have ADHD, compared to 2.5% in the general population. People with ADHD are **twice as likely** to work in interactive marketing. The creative industries aren't just inclusive of neurodivergent thinkers, they actively attract and depend on them.

Here's the problem: every time tracking tool on the market was designed as if this population doesn't exist.

## What executive function has to do with timesheets

Neurodivergence is a broad category encompassing ADHD, autism, dyslexia, dyspraxia, and other variations in how the brain processes information. What many of these conditions share, particularly ADHD, is a difference in executive function: the cognitive processes responsible for planning, organizing, initiating tasks, estimating time, and sustaining attention on low-interest activities.

A standard timesheet requires every single one of these capabilities:

- **Task initiation**: opening the tool and starting the entry (the hardest step for many with ADHD, initiating a boring task against competing interests)
- **Time estimation**: "how long did that take?" is a question that presumes accurate time perception, which ADHD directly impairs through a phenomenon clinicians call "time blindness"
- **Sequential processing**: navigating a multi-step form (select project, select task, enter hours, add notes, repeat for each block of time)
- **Sustained attention on low-interest tasks**: maintaining focus on a grid of numbers when the brain is screaming for stimulation

Time blindness deserves particular attention. It's not metaphorical. Changes in dopamine regulation make it genuinely harder for people with ADHD to perceive the passage of time, gauge how long tasks took, and distinguish between activities that felt short and activities that were short. Hyperfocus, the state where someone with ADHD becomes deeply absorbed in interesting work, can make three hours feel like thirty minutes. Asking that person to retrospectively estimate their time with any accuracy is asking their brain to do something it structurally can't.

As one time tracking blog noted directly: "If you have ADHD and work in an industry that uses timesheets to measure utilization, you probably have a tense relationship with your PM or accounting department."

## The masking burden

The Understood.org study found that **56%** of neurodivergent people in creative industries avoid discussing their neurodivergence at work, a higher rate than in other sectors. Only **19%** feel supported in their workplace, despite **69%** believing their thinking style gives them a creative advantage. At least **25%** have faced discrimination or bias related to their neurodivergence.

This creates a compounding problem for time tracking compliance. A designer with ADHD who struggles with timesheets can't explain why without disclosing a condition they may not feel safe disclosing. So instead of asking for help, they develop workarounds: filling out timesheets with invented numbers, batching weeks of entries in a single guilt-driven session, or relying on an EA to file for them (the proxy workflow).

The result looks like non-compliance. It gets treated as a performance or discipline issue. But it's a design issue. The tool demands cognitive capabilities that a significant portion of its users process differently.

## What clinical research suggests

The clinical literature on ADHD and time management converges on a principle that has direct implications for product design: **externalize time**.

When internal time perception is unreliable, the solution isn't to train people to perceive time better (this doesn't work, it's neurological, not behavioral). The solution is to create external representations of time that the person can observe, verify, and adjust.

This means:
- **Visual tools** rather than numeric grids (a pie chart is scannable; a spreadsheet of 15-minute increments is not)
- **Pre-populated data** rather than blank forms (an AI-drafted timesheet that shows "here's what we think your week looked like" eliminates the impossible task of recall)
- **Observable behavior** rather than self-reporting (calendar entries, Slack activity, and project tool usage provide an external record that doesn't depend on memory)
- **Confirmation rather than construction** ("does this look right?" is a fundamentally different cognitive task than "reconstruct your entire week from scratch")
- **Channel-based prompts** rather than app-based obligations (a Slack notification with a magic link removes the task initiation barrier, no app to open, no login to remember, no form to navigate)

Every one of these design principles, visual, pre-populated, observation-based, confirmation-oriented, channel-delivered, happens to also be better for neurotypical users. Designing for neurodivergence doesn't create a lesser product for everyone else. It creates a better product for everyone.

## The competitive advantage of neurodivergent teams

Research consistently shows that neurodivergent talent, properly supported, produces outsized creative value. Teams that include neurodivergent members can achieve **30% higher productivity** through different perspectives, pattern recognition abilities, and creative problem-solving approaches.

The creative industry intuitively understands this. Agencies hire for divergent thinking, associative reasoning, and the ability to see connections others miss. These are precisely the cognitive strengths associated with ADHD, autism, and dyslexia.

But then these same agencies impose administrative systems designed for sequential, detail-oriented, linear thinkers. The disconnect isn't just ironic, it's expensive. Every neurodivergent creative who spends an hour agonizing over a timesheet that a neurotypical colleague completes in ten minutes is an hour of creative capacity wasted. Multiply that across half your creative department, every week, and the cost adds up quickly.

And it's not just lost time. It's lost talent. Agency turnover runs 25-35% annually. When neurodivergent employees feel unsupported, when the daily experience of work includes systems that feel hostile to how their brain works, they leave. They go freelance, where they can manage their own time without forms. They go in-house, where the admin burden is lighter. They leave the industry entirely.

## Beyond accommodation

The traditional framing of neurodiversity in the workplace is accommodation: modifications made for a minority to function in systems designed for the majority. Ramps for wheelchair users. Screen readers for blind users. Extra time on exams for students with learning differences.

When **48%** of your workforce is neurodivergent, accommodation is the wrong frame. You're not modifying a system for a minority. You're recognizing that the system was designed for a population that represents barely half your users.

The correct frame is universal design: building systems that work for the full range of human cognitive variation from the start. Not as an add-on. Not as an accessibility feature. As the baseline.

A time tracking tool that relies on manual recall, sequential form-filling, and numeric data entry isn't a neutral tool that happens to disadvantage some users. It's a tool designed for a specific cognitive profile, and that profile doesn't describe nearly half the people who are supposed to use it.

## What this means for agency leadership

If you manage a creative agency, here's the implication: nearly half your creative team experiences your timesheet system as an executive function obstacle course. They're not being difficult. They're not being lazy. Their brains process time, task initiation, and sequential procedures differently.

You have two options. You can continue to enforce compliance through reminders, escalations, and penalties, approaches that create anxiety, erode trust, and drive the talent you most depend on toward the exit. Or you can adopt tools that meet your people where they actually are: visual, pre-filled, confirmation-based, and delivered through channels they already use.

The latter isn't just kinder. It produces better data, because a system people can actually use generates more accurate input than a system they avoid, game, or resent.

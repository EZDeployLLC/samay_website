# The 1,800-Hour Question: What Your CFO Actually Needs From Time Data

*Agency CFOs don't care about timesheet compliance. They care about whether 1,800 hours of creative capacity per person are being deployed against the right revenue. The data to answer that question doesn't exist at most agencies.*

---

Every full-time employee represents 2,080 available work hours per year -- 40 hours times 52 weeks. After accounting for vacation, holidays, sick time, training, and company meetings, the industry standard for billable capacity is approximately **1,800 hours per person per year**.

This isn't an arbitrary benchmark. It's the number that underpins how agencies price retainers, scope projects, plan staffing, and evaluate profitability. A senior art director billed at $175 per hour represents $315,000 in potential annual revenue. Whether the agency captures $280,000 or $220,000 of that potential depends entirely on how accurately those 1,800 hours are tracked, allocated, and billed.

At most agencies, the data to answer this question with any confidence simply does not exist.

## The five questions behind every timesheet

When a CFO asks about timesheets, they're not asking whether people filed on time. They're asking five questions that determine whether the agency is a sustainable business:

**1. Are we selling enough hours?**

Utilization -- the ratio of billable hours to available hours -- is the single most important operational metric in professional services. SPI Research's 2026 benchmark recorded billable utilization at **66.4%**, an all-time low in their 19-year survey history. At 1,800 available hours, 66.4% utilization means only 1,195 hours per person are being billed. That's 605 hours of capacity -- more than 15 full work weeks per employee -- sitting idle or going unrecorded.

But here's the question nobody asks: is utilization actually falling, or is it being measured against data that understates activity? If manual tracking captures only **67%** of billable work (WorkflowMax/Rize), then a team that appears to operate at 65% utilization might actually be closer to 85%. The people are working. The system just isn't capturing it.

**2. Are we pricing correctly?**

If a campaign actually consumed 120 hours but the team only logged 95, the agency estimates its cost at $16,625 (at $175/hour) when the real cost was $21,000. The margin looks 26% better than it is. The next similar campaign gets scoped at 95 hours because that's what the data shows. The under-pricing spiral begins.

**3. Where is the margin?**

Project profitability requires comparing actual hours against the fee collected. Without accurate time data, this calculation is impossible -- and agencies discover margin problems months after the invoice has gone out. Research shows agencies tracking project-level work-in-progress weekly identify unprofitable projects **4-6 weeks earlier** than monthly trackers, correlating with **8.7% higher overall margins**.

**4. Do we have the right people?**

Staffing mix -- the ratio of senior to mid-level to junior to freelance talent -- determines both output quality and cost structure. A campaign staffed with senior creatives at $175/hour has a fundamentally different margin profile than the same campaign delivered with mid-level talent at $120/hour plus a senior reviewer at 10 hours. Without time data by role, by project, by phase, the staffing model is a black box.

**5. What's our capacity?**

Can the agency take on a new client without hiring? The answer requires knowing actual utilization (not reported), current commitments, and historical patterns of how long similar work takes. Without real-time data, capacity planning is a gut call. With it, it's arithmetic.

## The fee decline makes this urgent

These questions have always mattered. What makes them existential now is the structural economics of the agency business.

Michael Farmer's analysis in *Madison Avenue Manslaughter* documented that agency fees have fallen **65% in real terms since 1995**. Holding company revenue has grown at just **1.6% per year** over the past 15 years -- below GDP growth. Meanwhile, the volume of deliverables has multiplied: more platforms, more formats, more versions, more markets, more content.

The math is stark: agencies are producing significantly more work for significantly less money. Every untracked hour, every undetected scope creep, every unrecovered expense compounds on a declining fee base. What was a tolerable leak at 1990s margins becomes a structural threat at today's.

Sir Martin Sorrell put it plainly: "The billable hour does not allow for any meaningful innovation." But the billable hour isn't going away. FunctionFox's 2025 survey found that **80%+** of agency revenue is still structured around hourly or time-based billing. The agencies that survive will be the ones that know, with precision, where those hours are going.

## The holding company squeeze

For agencies within holding company networks -- WPP, Omnicom, Publicis, Dentsu, IPG -- the pressure is even more direct. Holding companies push **15-16% operating margin targets** down to their agency brands. Agencies that can demonstrate precise utilization, project margins, and resource allocation have data to defend their position in portfolio reviews. Those that can't are restructuring targets.

The data holding companies demand is the same data that would help any independent agency run better. Utilization by team. Margin by client. Capacity by quarter. Scope creep by project type. This isn't top-down surveillance -- it's operational intelligence that any well-run services business needs.

But producing this data requires accurate, real-time time tracking. And the agencies under the most margin pressure are often the same ones whose overworked teams are least likely to file timesheets accurately. The cycle reinforces itself.

## The over-servicing trap

FunctionFox's 2025 Creative Agency Industry Report found that **79% of agencies routinely work beyond scope** without additional compensation. An Ignition survey of 273 services firm managers found that **57% lose $1,000 to $5,000 per month** to unbilled out-of-scope work. Only **1%** of agencies successfully bill for all out-of-scope work.

Ninety-nine percent are giving away labor. Not occasionally. Routinely.

Michael Farmer's prescription: "Establishing a policy that the agency will be paid for all the work it does -- eliminating unpaid out-of-scope work -- will add at least **10% to revenue** and increase profits accordingly." For a $25 million agency, that's $2.5 million from a single operational change.

But you can't bill for scope creep you can't see. And you can't see scope creep without real-time data comparing actual hours consumed against the scoped estimate. By the time the project ships and someone in finance tallies the write-offs, the conversation with the client about a change order is three months too late.

## From compliance to resource intelligence

The reframe that changes the conversation: timesheets aren't a compliance requirement. They're a **resource intelligence system**.

Every hour tracked is a data point about how the agency's most expensive asset -- creative talent -- is being deployed. Aggregate those data points across people, projects, clients, and time periods, and patterns emerge that are invisible at the individual timesheet level:

- **The client consuming 30% more hours than it pays for** -- detectable only when actual hours are compared to scope across quarters, not individual weeks.
- **The team approaching 115% capacity** -- visible only when utilization is tracked in real time, not estimated in monthly reports.
- **The project type that consistently overruns** -- identifiable only when historical actuals are compared to estimates across similar engagements.
- **The senior creative approaching burnout** -- signaled by sustained overtime patterns, declining response times, and increasing after-hours activity over 14+ days.

None of these insights come from a single timesheet. They emerge from the aggregate -- from having enough accurate data, over enough time, to see patterns. And they're worth orders of magnitude more than the compliance data the timesheet was designed to produce.

## The resource management view

The most operationally sophisticated agencies are beginning to treat time data the way financial firms treat portfolio data. Not "did they file?" but "how is this asset performing?"

A resource management view puts the 1,800-hour benchmark at the center:

- **Per person:** 1,800 hours available. How many are billable? How many are allocated to active projects? How many are unassigned? Is this person underutilized (a revenue problem) or over-allocated (a retention risk)?
- **Per project:** How many hours were planned? How many consumed? What's the burn rate against the fee? Is this engagement on track to be profitable, or should the account team start the change-order conversation now?
- **Per client:** What's the total investment in creative hours over the past year? Does the fee structure reflect the real cost of delivery? Are scope boundaries holding?
- **Per agency:** What's the blended utilization rate? How does it compare to the **75-80% target** that top-quartile agencies achieve (SPI Research)? Where is under-utilized capacity, and can it absorb new business without adding headcount?

## Why the data has to come from a different kind of tool

Traditional time tracking tools generate the wrong kind of data for resource intelligence. When timesheets are filed from memory, days or weeks late, with rough allocations -- the patterns they produce are noise, not signal.

The data quality threshold for resource management is substantially higher than for basic compliance. Compliance asks: "did they file?" Resource intelligence asks: "is the data accurate enough to base staffing, pricing, and capacity decisions on?"

Useful resource data requires approximately **85% accuracy and 90% compliance** across the organization. Manual time tracking, filed from memory, typically achieves **47-66% accuracy** (AffinityLive). The gap isn't incremental. It requires a fundamentally different approach to capture.

That approach is observation-first: drafting timesheets from calendar data, meeting attendance, project tool activity, and communication patterns. Presenting a pre-filled summary for human review. Reducing the task from reconstruction to confirmation. Delivering the draft through channels people already use, so compliance becomes a 30-second interaction rather than a 30-minute ordeal.

When data quality improves, the resource management view becomes possible. When the resource management view becomes possible, the executive conversation changes entirely.

## The conversation shift

**Before accurate time data:**
- "Timesheets are a compliance problem."
- "How do we get people to file?"
- "We need to chase harder."

**After:**
- "We're over-serving this client by 22%. Let's restructure the retainer."
- "The design team hits 115% capacity in three weeks if we take this pitch. Budget a freelancer now or push the timeline."
- "Our actual cost per deliverable is $4,200, not the $3,100 we've been quoting. Our pricing needs to catch up."
- "Two senior creatives have logged 50+ hour weeks for six consecutive weeks. Redistribute before we lose them."

That's the shift from compliance to intelligence. From counting hours to managing resources. From "did they file?" to "are we deploying our $150,000-per-head investment where it generates the most value?"

The CFO doesn't care about timesheets. The CFO cares about the 1,800-hour question. And answering it starts with data that's accurate enough to trust.
